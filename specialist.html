FLIM for Cancer Surgery

NAD(P)H as an Indicator of Metabolism

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP+) are electron carriers crucial to human metabolism. They exist in equilibrium with their reduced form, NADH and NADPH respectively. Together, NADH and NADPH are called NAD(P)H (1). NAD(P)H and cancer metabolism

Compared to normal cells, cancer cells preferentially use glycolysis rather than oxidative phosphorylation for ATP production despite the presence of oxygen, which is known as the Warburg effect (2), The Warburg effect is one of the phenomenon that shows cancer cells modify their metabolism to support proliferation. Although the Warburg effect was rationalised by benefits such as promoting ATP synthesis and biosynthesis, it does not fully explain the metabolism in cancer cell (3).

The Warburg effect is characterised by increased glycolytic flux and increased NAD+ demand, which is supported by mechanisms including malate-aspartate shuttle and lactate fermentation (4). Furthermore, adaptation of cancer cells also include shift in biosynthetic pathway and reactive oxygen species production (5), so both NADH and NADPH level are affected.

Moreira et al. found that the [NAD+]/[NADH] and [NADP+]/[NADPH] ratios are increased approximately fivefold and tenfold in cancer cells relative to normal cells (6).

Potential rationale:

  • In order to maintain high glycolytic rate in cancer cells, pyruvate needs to be rapidly removed, while NAD+ needs to be rapidly replenished. This is achieved by the expression of lactate dehydrogenase, which couples the reduction of pyruvate with the oxidation of NADH, resulting in a high [NAD+]/[NADH] ratio that maintains the glycolytic flux (7).
  • The high [NADP+]/[NADPH] ratio can be attributed to the increased use of NADPH as a reducing agent for generation of antioxidant defence and anabolic reactions such as nucleotide, amino acid and fatty acid synthesis (8).

Distinguish cancer tissue from normal tissue

Although the Warburg effect is not a universal phenomenon for all cancer types, it has been shown consistently that the metabolism and microenvironment cancer cells is significantly different from normal cells. These affect the properties of fluorescence molecules.

Since NAD(P)H is intrinsically fluorescent (9), whereas its oxidised forms are not, fluorescence imaging allows the redox state to be assessed without using any exogenous fluorescence probes. Research has shown that brain tumour tissues emit lower autofluorescence intensity than normal tissues, so fluorescence imaging could allow delineation of tumour resection margin (10-12). However, the clinical application of this intensity-based approach is constrained by significant limitations:

  1. Non-uniform illumination artefacts: The fluorescence image is bright at the centre, but intensity drops at the periphery due to uneven illumination of endoscope (12)
  2. Low sensitivity: NAD(P)H has low quantum yield, so fluorescence image has low signal to noise ratio due to background fluorescence (13)
  3. Haemoglobin absorption: fluorescence intensity signals are attenuated by haemoglobin, so blood vessels in tissue may affect discrimination between tumour and normal tissue (14)

Application of FLIM in cancer surgery

In contrast to fluorescence intensity measurements, fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM) provides a robust metric for delineating tumour resection margin.

Fluorescence lifetime is the duration in which a fluorophore stays in its excited state before returning to the ground state. The fluorescence lifetime of a fluorophore is largely determined by the non-radiative decay rate, in other words, the restraint of molecular motion. For example, low temperature, high viscosity or a protein-bound state hinders non-radiative relaxation, thereby increases the fluorescence lifetime. Therefore, fluorescence lifetime is very sensitive to microenvironment and metabolic states.

The basics of FLIM are discussed in the Science for non-specialists page.

It has been demonstrated that FLIM enabled discrimination between glioblastoma and normal cortex with a high statistical significance (p = 0.0027), more robust than intensity-based imaging (p = 0.0199) (12).

The robustness of FLIM is largely attributed to the fact that fluorescence lifetime is an intrinsic property, independent of NAD(P)H concentration and the detected intensity (15). Therefore, fluorescence lifetime remains uniform across the field of view, unaffected by background lighting.

Furthermore, fluorescence lifetime is influenced by various parameters that differentiate tumour from normal tissues. For example, the free and protein-bound states of NAD(P)H have distinct fluorescence lifetimes, and the free to bound ratio of NADH is linked to [NAD+]/[NADH] ratio (16, 17). In addition, the fluorescence lifetime of enzyme-bound NADH and NADPH are different and the lifetimes depend on enzyme binding configuration and which enzyme NAD(P)H is bound (18, 19). Therefore, the metabolic and environmental change in cancer compared to normal tissue results in the measurable difference in fluorescence lifetime, making FLIM a sensitive surgical tool.
