Science for Non-specialists

What is NAD(P)H?

NADH and NADPH are electron carriers in cellular metabolism that serve different roles. NADH is mainly involved in processes that break down glucose and yield energy. NADPH is mainly involved in antioxidation and biosynthesis such as production of nucleotides and fatty acids. They are recycled from their oxidised forms, NAD+ and NADP+ respectively. Due to their roles in multiple pathways, they reflect cellular metabolism.

NADH and NADPH are very similar in structure, the only difference between two molecules is just a single phosphate group. They both absorb ultraviolet light and emit fluorescence. (NAD+ and NADP+ do not). Their fluorescence property is contributed by the nicotinamide chromophore. Since their differing phosphate group is not in proximity to the nicotinamide chromophore, their absorption and emission spectra are almost identical (9, 20, 21). Therefore, NADH and NADPH are often abbreviated to NAD(P)H in the context of fluorescence imaging (1).

What is fluorescence?

When a fluorescent molecule (fluorophore) absorbs a photon at a specific excitation wavelength, it transitions from ground state to excited states. The fluorophore may return to the ground state through radiative decay by emitting a photon with longer wavelength (fluorescence), or through non-radiative decay, which releases the energy without emitting a photon.

What is FLIM?

Fluorescence lifetime is the average time in which a fluorophore spent in its excited state before emitting fluorescence. Fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM) works like a stopwatch, which measures the time delay between a laser pulse and the detection of the resulting fluorescence (Fig A). It takes multiple measurements to generate a histogram that shows photon count as a function of time (Fig B). The fluorescence lifetime is determined by fitting the histogram with an exponential decay. A FLIM image is a pseudo-color map where each pixel represents an average fluorescence lifetime.

In general, fluorescence lifetime is sensitive to pH, viscosity, ion concentration and temperature in the local environment.

The fluorescence lifetime of NAD(P)H

  • NADH and NADPH have the same fluorescence lifetime (0.4 ns) when they are free in solution
  • Enzyme-bound NADPH has longer fluorescence lifetime than enzyme-bound NADH (4.4 ns and 1.5 ns)
  • Fluorescence lifetime of enzyme-bound NAD(P)H depends on enzyme binding configuration and which enzyme it binds (18, 19)
  • The free to bound ratio of NADH is linked to [NAD+]/[NADH] ratio (16, 17)

Tumour margin delineation with FLIM

Cancer cells reprogramme their metabolism to support rapid cell proliferation. It leads to significant changes in cellular redox states, composition of enzymes that bind NAD(P)H and the free to bound ratio of NAD(P)H. Therefore, the fluorescence lifetimes of cancer and normal tissues are significantly different. This allows FLIM to delineate tumour margin, thereby guiding tumour resection in surgery.
